I would build this around one principle: the agent prepares the evidence; the analyst owns the interpretation. Success means analysts spend less time chasing information and more time understanding companies—and can still explain every number and conclusion they submit.
Treat the following as a proposed product blueprint, with the Fund setting the final rules.
1. Define the boundary around learning
“Research” needs a clearer boundary than “writing,” because an agent could effectively do the assignment through a detailed research summary even if the analyst types the final email.
Give analysts an evidence workspace, rather than a draft-update button. Organize it around source documents, extracted facts, charts, unresolved questions, and analyst notes.
2. Major movements: detect → notify → investigate
This is the best first feature because the trigger is measurable and the analyst’s responsibility remains clear.
First, define “±4% compared to SPX.” My suggested interpretation is a difference of four percentage points between the holding’s daily return and SPX’s daily return:
- Holding +5.0%; SPX +0.7% → relative move +4.3 points → trigger.
- Holding −3.5%; SPX +0.8% → relative move −4.3 points → trigger.
The Fund should also decide whether an intraday breach counts or only the closing return. A useful design is intraday heads-ups with the official requirement determined at the close, if that matches Fund policy.
The agent would:
1. Monitor holdings against SPX using aligned timestamps and trading sessions.
2. Notify the responsible team when the rule is met.
3. Open a research workspace containing the price move, company announcements, relevant news, peer movements, and source timestamps.
4. Assign an owner and track the update deadline.
5. Remind the team if the update is outstanding.
The notification can be a fixed factual template: ticker, stock return, benchmark return, relative move, timestamp, and workspace link. It is separate from the analyst-written major movement email to the Fund.
For initial research, distinguish “potentially relevant developments” from a verified explanation. The agent should be comfortable reporting that it found no clear catalyst.
3. Earnings: automate preparation around the analyst’s process
Each company gets a reusable earnings checklist and chart package, initially approved by its team.
Before earnings, the agent can:
- Track the date and whether it is company-confirmed or estimated.
- Remind the team according to Fund deadlines.
- Gather the prior release, guidance, presentation, and the team’s previous update.
- Refresh approved charts using available historical data.
- Bring forward questions analysts recorded last quarter.
The analyst supplies the expectations, key questions, and assessment of what would change the thesis.
After earnings, the agent can:
- Retrieve the release, presentation, filing, and transcript as each becomes available.
- Populate a sourced table of actual results, prior-year results, prior guidance, and estimates where available.
- Refresh the same charts.
- Flag changed metric definitions, missing disclosures, and inconsistent figures.
- Link relevant management comments to questions the analyst entered before earnings.
The analyst explains the results, updates assumptions, and writes the post-earnings update.
A particularly useful learning feature: preserve the analyst’s pre-earnings expectations and ask them to revisit those expectations afterward. That makes the platform support reflection as well as efficiency.
4. Model historicals: automate repeated extraction after analysts establish the mapping
This needs the most care. Copying an established number repeatedly is tedious; deciding what a reported number means and where it belongs is part of learning accounting and modeling.
I would use this workflow:
1. Analyst establishes the first period. They locate the source figures and explain how company-reported lines map into the model.
2. The agent proposes the remaining historicals. It uses the approved mapping and shows the source for every proposed input.
3. The analyst reviews exceptions. Changed reporting, ambiguous mappings, restatements, and new segments require attention.
4. Approved inputs enter the model. Preserve formulas and keep a record of changes.
Every proposed number should carry its period, units, currency, reported label, source location, and any calculation used to derive it.
Avoid forcing every company into one identical mapping. Maintain a company-specific mapping and flag when disclosures change. Particular review points include:
- Quarterly versus year-to-date figures.
- Fiscal versus calendar periods.
- GAAP versus adjusted measures.
- Segment changes and restated comparatives.
- Sign conventions and units.
For learning, use demonstrated proficiency to unlock more automation. New analysts complete a small historical section manually and explain it; experienced analysts can approve larger batches. Sampling a few inputs back to their filings keeps source familiarity without recreating all the busywork.
5. Six team agents, with shared Fund infrastructure
Give each team its own agent experience and research context, backed by common tools and Fund policies.
This avoids six agents independently collecting the same information or applying different definitions of a major movement.
For cross-team questions, a person could ask another team’s agent: “What has your team documented about freight costs?”
The answer should identify:
- What comes from public sources.
- What comes from that team’s approved research.
- When the material was last updated.
- Whether it is a working view or an approved team position.
An agent should not invent the other team’s opinion or present its own inference as that team’s conclusion. Unanswered questions can be routed to a person. Access should follow shared-folder permissions; talking to another agent should not unlock private notes.
A useful additional benefit is continuity between semesters: incoming analysts inherit sourced research history, model mappings, and unresolved questions, while still developing their own thesis.
6. Build in stages
Pilot with one team first. For historicals, test on the same company across several periods before expanding to other reporting structures.
Keep the first release narrow: a holdings roster, a dependable movement trigger, an alert, a sourced evidence page, and an analyst completion status.
7. Measure both efficiency and learning
Track time spent gathering data, missed deadlines, false or duplicate alerts, source accuracy, and corrections to historical inputs.
Alongside those, have team leads periodically check whether analysts can:
- Trace a figure to its filing.
- Explain a model mapping.
- Defend their explanation of an event.
- Describe how evidence changed—or did not change—the thesis.
Less manual work is only a win if understanding holds up.
The most useful next planning exercise is to walk through one past major movement, one earnings cycle, and one historical model section. Label each step automate, assist, or analyst-owned. Those real examples will give you a much stronger first specification than starting with a broad agent chat interface.
